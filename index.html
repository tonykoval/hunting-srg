A Series of Three Hunt Logs Hunting Strongly Regular Graphs

A strongly regular graph is one of the most rigid objects in combinatorics: $n$ vertices, every vertex of degree $k$, every edge in $\lambda$ triangles, every non-edge with $\mu$ common neighbours. For three particular parameter sets, a simple question has no answer. These are their hunt logs.

One question, three shapes

The same object poses three genuinely different questions, and this series follows each to the edge of what is known:

SRG(37,18,8,9) — completeness

A catalog of $6802$ conference graphs is known. Is it complete, or is there a $6803$rd? The target would be rigid, so symmetry is no help; the hunt is an isomorph-free enumeration of regular two-graphs on $38$ points. Status: $>92\%$ of the space proven empty, a hard residual remaining.

SRG(69,20,7,5) — existence

The smallest parameters in Brouwer's table with no known graph and no non-existence proof. Does even one exist? Status: no automorphism of any odd order, no Cayley graph; the involution sweep and the rigid case remain.

SRG(99,14,1,2) — Conway's $\$1000$ problem

John Conway's famous $99$-graph: every edge in a unique triangle, every non-edge in a unique quadrilateral. Does it exist? Status: orders $11$ and $3$ closed, not Cayley; the order-$7$ door $[1,7^{14}]$ and the rigid core remain.

The shared lesson

The iron law

All three targets resist every general method for the same reason. A strongly regular graph that is both large and nearly symmetry-free forces full enumeration cost: symmetry-based methods (orbit matrices, Cayley/PDS, character sums) reach only symmetric graphs, while raw search (SAT, local search, geometric filters) drowns at low symmetry. The two tractable regimes — small, or highly symmetric — are exactly the ones these problems avoid. What is left is slow, sound, isomorph-free enumeration, and the precise map of where the wall stands.

How to read

Each volume is a standalone interactive book — plain static HTML, KaTeX for the mathematics, live calculators and visualisers, no build step. Open any of the three in the sidebar (rendered editions on GitHub Pages; source on GitHub). Every result is reproducible: the search engine, orbit-gen, and the exact commands are catalogued in each book's Tools & Reproducibility chapter.

Two ways every hunt ends

For each problem the search is a decision procedure: a single valid graph settles it one way (a new graph, a resolved existence, or Conway's prize), and a complete exhaustion settles it the other (a completeness theorem or a non-existence proof). Neither has been reached — yet. That is what makes them worth hunting, and worth writing down.
